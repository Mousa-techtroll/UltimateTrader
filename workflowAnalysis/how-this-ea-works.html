How the UltimateTrader gold bot works

It's an automated trader that watches the price of gold and, once an hour, decides whether to buy — and if it does, it manages that trade from start to finish, sizing it so a wrong guess costs only a small, fixed slice of the account.

Written for someone brand new to trading — every term is defined the first time it appears. It describes what the bot actually does on its default settings, and explains mechanics only: there are no profit, win-rate, or "how much money" claims anywhere. Each "Exact rule" box gives the precise condition behind the plain-English explanation, so you can skim the story and dip into the specifics whenever you want.

The big picture

1One decision, once an hour

The bot works on the 1-hour candle — a "candle" is one block on a price chart that records where price opened, closed, and the high and low it hit during that hour. It only acts when an hour finishes, so it never reacts to a half-formed price that might still reverse.

Like a careful shopper who checks the shelf at the top of each hour instead of fidgeting over every flicker, the bot runs the same routine every hour: read the market, look for the single best trade idea, run that idea through a wall of safety checks, size it, place it, then manage it until it closes. Step one each hour

2How it reads the market

Before it thinks about any specific trade, the bot "reads the room." That picture has three parts — each answers a question a human trader asks first.

a) "Which direction am I even allowed to go?" — the bias

Gold has spent years in a long, grinding climb. The bot is built around that: it is heavily tilted toward buying ("going long"), and a buy must clear a thorough set of checks — is price above its long-term average line, does the trend agree, does the current market type agree? Selling ("going short") is treated as the rare, suspicious case.

Why a trader cares: trading with the prevailing direction is like paddling downstream instead of fighting the current. You can still be wrong, but the odds are friendlier.

b) "What kind of market is this right now?" — the regime

The bot sorts the market into a type: Trending (a clear staircase one way), Ranging (bouncing inside a box, going nowhere), Choppy (messy, indecisive, lots of fakeouts), or Volatile (lurching in big fast swings). This label changes how boldly it acts — it risks a bit more in a clean trend and shrinks right down in choppy or volatile conditions.

Why a trader cares: the same idea is smart in a trend and reckless in chop. A good trader asks "is this kind of market one where my style even works?" — and the bot turns the dial down when things look treacherous.

c) "What time of day is it?" — the session

Gold trades almost around the clock, and it behaves differently depending on which part of the world is awake. The bot tracks three sessions — Asia, London, and New York — and, for example, deliberately takes half its usual risk in London (historically a rougher window for this style) and a little less in New York.

Why a trader cares: a thin, quiet market moves erratically and costs more to trade; a busy one is smoother. Knowing when you trade matters as much as what.

Once-a-week ruleThe bot will not open a brand-new trade on a Friday — it doesn't want to be caught holding a fresh position over the weekend, when the market is closed and price can "gap" (jump) on Monday. UltimateTrader.mq5:1585

Finding a trade

3The entry engines — many scouts, one decision

Picture the bot as a hiring manager who, once an hour, interviews a panel of specialist scouts. Each scout is an expert in one chart shape or situation. The manager asks them all "do you see your setup right now?", scores every "yes," lines them up best-to-worst, and hires exactly one — the highest-scoring idea. Everyone else's idea is dropped until next hour.

That "interview everyone, then pick the single best" step is what makes this safe to run with many strategies at once: they don't all fire independently — they compete, and one winner is chosen.

~12 scouts are polled every hour — "do you see your setup?"
▼
Each "yes" is scored 0–10 and graded a tier: A+ / A / B+ / B (or rejected)
▼
The bot keeps only the single highest-scoring idea
▼
One trade (sometimes after a 1-hour confirmation wait)
"Chaining" = poll all scouts → grade → keep the best one. Usually only one trade opens per hour.

The grade decides how much it risks

A higher grade means the bot is more confident, so it's allowed to risk a little more money on that trade (more on sizing in section 5). "Risk X%" means: if this trade hits its safety exit, the account loses about X% — not that it bets X%.

Exact ruleA 0–10 score is built from trend agreement, regime fit, macro alignment, pattern type and a choppiness check, then mapped to a tier: A+ ≥ 8, A ≥ 7, B+ ≥ 6, else rejected. The tier sets the base risk: A+ = 1.5%, A = 1.0%, B+ = 0.75%, B = 0.6%. A "none" grade is thrown out before it can take a slot. CSetupEvaluator.mqh:170-350 · UltimateTrader_Inputs.mqh:45-48
Exact rule — the 1-hour confirmationSome winning ideas wait one more hour and only enter if the next candle keeps going the same way (closes past the pattern). It's on by default, but deliberately skipped for short trades and mean-reversion trades, and several scouts opt out and trade immediately (S3, S6, Session, Expansion). CSignalOrchestrator.mqh:878-961

Terms: long = a bet price goes up (buy); short = a bet price goes down (sell); mean-reversion = betting price snaps back after overshooting; ATR = a yardstick for how much gold typically moves in an hour; wick = the thin line above/below a candle's body marking a rejected extreme.

The simple "candle-shape" scouts

Engulfing Long only

A single up-candle that completely "swallows" the previous down-candle — buyers decisively overpowering sellers, like one runner lapping another.

Exact rule: prior candle down, signal candle up and engulfs its body (≥ 80% of the prior body) and is > 0.3×ATR → buy at market. The short side is off by default. CEngulfingEntry.mqh:160-211

Pin Bar Both (short NY-blocked)

A candle with a long "tail" and a small body — price stabbed one way and got slapped back. A long lower tail = buyers rejected lower prices.

Exact rule: lower wick > 1.5× the body, upper wick < 0.8× the body, close in the top 30% of the range → buy. Bearish pin is the mirror but blocked during New York hours. CPinBarEntry.mqh:159-244

MA Cross Long only

A "moving average" is a smoothed running average of price. A fast 10-bar average crossing above a slow 20-bar average is a classic "momentum just turned up" signal.

Exact rule: the 10-MA was at/below the 20-MA last hour and is above it now (a fresh upward cross) → buy. Trending regime only, and blocked during New York. CMACrossEntry.mqh:173-217

Displacement Both

Price sweeps past a recent low to grab orders, then a big decisive candle drives back the other way — institutions showing their hand. A quick fake-out dip, then a forceful shove up.

Exact rule: a wick dips below the recent swing low but closes back above it (a "sweep"), then a candle with a body ≥ 1.5×ATR closes decisively above that level → buy. Mirror for sells. CDisplacementEntry.mqh:202-256

The "range" scouts (the S3 / S6 pair)

RangeEdgeFade — S3 Both

When gold is stuck bouncing inside a box, price often pokes just outside and snaps back — a "false break." S3 buys that snap-back at the floor (or sells it at the ceiling), betting price returns to the middle. Like a ball that briefly rolls off a table edge and gets caught.

Exact rule: inside a validated range box, the low pierced just below the floor but closed back inside, while at the lower edge or oversold; reward to the far edge must be ≥ 2× the risk → buy. CRangeEdgeFade.mqh:148-221

FailedBreakReversal — S6 Long only (short off)

Price spikes past an important level to grab the orders resting there, then reclaims it — a textbook "stop hunt" that fails. Like a wave that crashes over a seawall then drains right back behind it. (The most carefully-specified scout in the system.)

Exact rule: price spiked below a level by ≥ 0.20×ATR; the wick that pierced is bigger than the candle body (proof of rejection); price then closes back above the level by a margin, but not so far that the snap-back is exhausted → buy. Short side is off by default. CFailedBreakReversal.mqh:126-191

The "breakout" scouts

VolatilityBreakout Both — effectively dormant

Price punching out of its recent 20-bar range and a volatility band while the bigger trend agrees — a momentum breakout.

Exact rule: volatile regime + ADX ≥ 25 + trend agreement + a close beyond the 20-bar high or the upper volatility band. Its volatile-only filter produces essentially zero trades by design — kept for parity. CVolatilityBreakoutEntry.mqh:148-281

CrashBreakout Short only

Only wakes up in a confirmed gold downtrend ("death cross" = the 50-day average below the 200-day). When price rubber-bands too far above its short-term mean inside that downtrend, it shorts the snap-back. Like a stretched elastic expected to recoil.

Exact rule: daily death cross present, then price > (1-hour EMA21 + 2×ATR) and ADX ≥ 25 → sell, targeting the EMA21 mean. CCrashBreakoutEntry.mqh:189-272

The engines (multi-mode scouts)

An engine is a scout that checks several related setups ("modes") in priority order and reports at most one idea — the first mode that triggers. Four engines run by default:

Liquidity engine Both

Checks four "smart-money" modes in order — Displacement → Order-Block Retest → Fair-Value-Gap Mitigation → Swing-Failure — and returns the first that fires. These hunt the zones where big institutional orders likely sit (price returning to an "order block," filling a price "gap," or sweeping a swing and reversing), each needing a confluence score to agree.

Exact rule: e.g. Order-Block Retest = price re-enters a prior institutional candle's zone, recent structure is bullish, and a rejection candle forms. Stands aside on news days; on volatile days only Displacement is allowed. CLiquidityEngine.mqh:352-1127

Session engine Both

Trades the daily clock. Two modes are on: London Breakout (early London, price breaks out of the overnight Asian range) and NY Continuation (at the New York open, price extends London's move). The Asian phase only builds a range; two other modes ship off.

Exact rule: London Breakout (GMT 8–10) = candle opens below the Asian-session high but closes above it by 0.35×ATR with the trend up. Each mode only fires inside its GMT hour window. CSessionEngine.mqh:85-877

Expansion engine Both

Looks for energy building then releasing. Institutional-Candle Breakout (one oversized candle, then a few quiet bars coiling inside it, then a break out) and Compression Breakout (a volatility "squeeze" that releases with momentum).

Exact rule: Institutional candle = body ≥ 2×ATR closing near its extreme, ≥ 2 coil bars, then a close beyond the big candle's high; stop at its low, targets 1R and 2R. CExpansionEngine.mqh:693-1000

PullbackContinuation engine Both

Buys the dip inside an uptrend (or sells the bounce in a downtrend) — the bread-and-butter "trend pullback." Like a car climbing a hill that briefly coasts back, then accelerates again.

Exact rule: 4-hour trend up with ADX ≥ 18, price pulled back 0.6–1.8 ATR over 2–10 bars showing exhaustion, then a reclaim candle (body ≥ 0.35×ATR) closes back above the prior two highs → buy; target 1.3R. CPullbackContinuationEngine.mqh:375-565

Before any order is placed

4The safety-check gauntlet

Spotting a possible trade is only the beginning. The idea then has to pass a wall of checks — like airport security with many gates. One "no" at any gate and the trade is cancelled for now.

  1. Is the kill switch on? If the operator flipped the master "stop everything" switch, the bot does nothing.
  2. Is trading halted for the day? If a daily safety limit already tripped, no new trade. CRiskMonitor — daily-loss / max-trades halt
  3. Is the market spiking abnormally? On a sudden violent shock, the bot stands aside rather than trade a spike it can't trust.
  4. Is this a low-quality time to trade? If the session looks too thin or sloppy to execute well, the trade is blocked or its risk trimmed.
  5. Is the cost of trading too high? If the "spread" (the buy-vs-sell price gap you pay) is too wide, reject. InpMaxSpreadPoints = 50
  6. Is the market flip-flopping? If the market type changed more than twice in 4 hours — whipsaw chaos — it waits it out.
  7. Is this an allowed session and hour? The trade must fall inside the permitted windows.
  8. Does the idea pass its own quality vetting? Direction/trend/market-type checks, plus a minimum quality grade — anything too weak is thrown out.
  9. Are we already holding too many trades? At most 5 open at once. InpMaxPositions = 5
  10. Is the stop a safe distance from the noise? The protective stop must sit at least 3× the spread away. InpMinSLToSpreadRatio = 3.0
  11. Is the reward worth the risk? The potential gain must be at least 1.3× the amount risked. InpMinRRRatio = 1.3
  12. Would it breach the total-risk cap? Across all open trades, money-at-risk can't exceed 5% of the account; a trade that would bust that is shrunk to fit or rejected. InpMaxTotalExposure = 5.0
One gate that ships OFFThere is also a news filter meant to make the bot stand flat around big scheduled announcements (like interest-rate decisions). On the default settings it is disabled — it only switches on alongside another optional module that is also off — so by default the bot does not stand aside for news. CMarketContext.mqh:1031

How big is each trade?

5Money management — position sizing

Imagine deciding, before you sit at a casino table, "tonight I'll lose at most $100 here" — then choosing chip sizes to fit. The bot sizes every trade that way, in a strict order.

1. It first decides the dollars it's willing to lose on this one trade — a small slice of the account (the tier's risk %). On a $10,000 account, 1% = $100.  2. It looks at the stop-loss distance — how far the "I was wrong" exit sits from entry.  3. It works backwards to a position size so that if the stop is hit, the loss equals that pre-decided amount — not a penny more.

The key consequence: a wider stop forces a smaller position; a tighter stop allows a bigger one. The dollar risk stays the same either way — like risking $100 on a bet, but putting down fewer chips when each chip is riskier.

Exact rule — the sizing formulalots = risk_dollars ÷ (stop_distance × value-per-point), where risk_dollars = balance × risk% and the value-per-point is read live from the broker (so the math is in real dollars for whatever symbol you're on). The result is rounded down to the broker's step and clamped to its min/max. A trade needing > 80% of free margin is refused. CQualityTierRiskStrategy.mqh:314-421

That base % is only the starting point. A set of situational dials then nudge it — almost always down — before the final size is computed: the session dial (London ×0.5, NY ×0.9), the regime dial (trend ×1.25, choppy ×0.6, volatile ×0.75), an automatic drawdown brake that eases off after a cold streak (down to ×0.70, never above 1.0), and a counter-trend halving (×0.5) when a trade fights the big-picture direction. Whatever the dials do, the hard cap in the next section is the last word.

Dials that ship off / inertA consecutive-loss scaler is wired on but is a near-no-op in practice; a short-protection dial is set to ×1.0 (no effect); a Wednesday reducer and a trend-quality boost are off. None change live behavior.

The circuit breakers

6Risk management — the safety rails

The dials adjust size. These limits are absolute stops that no setup quality or dial can override — the speed governor and emergency brake.

2%
Max risk per trade
No single trade may risk more than 2% of the account — the final risk is clamped down to it. Enforced twice.
−3%
Daily-loss halt
If account equity drops 3% below the day's start, all new trading stops until the next day. Because it watches equity, an open trade bleeding against you can trip it in real time. The single most important protection.
5
Max open positions
Never more than 5 trades at once (and at most 5 opened per day).
5%
Total-risk cap
Across all open trades, combined money-at-risk can't exceed 5% — five same-way gold trades are really one big bet. A new trade is shrunk to fit, or rejected.

Two more backstops: a consecutive-error halt (5 failed orders in a row stops trading — a sign something's broken), and the master emergency kill switch.

What the bot will not do

Two infamous ways traders blow up accounts: martingale ("double down after a loss" — bet $100, lose, bet $200, then $400, until one streak wipes you out) and grids (drop a ladder of orders and keep adding as price runs against you). This bot was specifically audited and confirmed to do neither.

Exact rule — confirmed absentIt never adds to a losing trade, never increases size after a loss (every result-based dial can only multiply by ≤ 1.0 — after a losing streak it bets smaller), never widens a stop-loss (the stop only ever ratchets toward safety), and runs no grid (entries are discrete — at most one new trade per hour). design-audit DT-1 §20 — scored 5/5

After the trade opens

7Exits & the trailing system

Once a trade is open, this is where the bot earns its keep. First a term you'll need: R = the amount risked on the trade (the entry-to-stop distance, in dollars). If a stop-out would lose $100, then 1R = $100, a "2R win" made $200, and milestones like "0.7R" mean price has travelled 70% of a risk-unit — in the profit direction.

A winning trade's life: bank profit in slices, get to break-even fast, then let a runner ride behind a stop that only ever tightens.

The stop-loss — set before the trade even opens

The bot picks the price at which it will admit "this idea is dead" before entering, and puts it just past the spot where the trade's logic would be proven wrong — e.g. just below the low of the bullish pattern it bought. Because gold can wiggle $3–$5 for no reason, the stop is also pushed out to a minimum distance so normal "noise" can't trigger it; it uses the wider of {structural stop, noise floor}, and once set, the stop is never widened.

Exact ruleStop = the further-away of {just beyond the pattern, entry − minimum floor}; minimum floor InpMinSLPoints = 800 points = $8.00 on gold. Never widened — every update must be strictly safer or it's skipped. CEngulfingEntry.mqh:179 · CPositionCoordinator.mqh:2819-2893

The take-profit ladder — banking profit in stages

Rather than holding everything and hoping for the top, the bot sells in slices at planned milestones (like selling some apples now, some higher, some higher still, keeping a few to ride). What's left after the last rung is the runner — a small piece left open to chase a big trend, protected by the trailing stop.

Exact rule — the ladder (all measured in R)TP0 at 0.7R closes ~15% · TP1 at 1.3R closes ~40% of the rest · TP2 at 1.8R closes ~30% of the rest · the runner (no fixed target) trails. UltimateTrader_Inputs.mqh:446-448,129-132

Break-even — making a winner impossible to lose

Once the trade has a decent cushion, the bot slides the stop up to roughly the entry price. From then on the worst likely outcome is about a scratch — like locking a safety clamp behind you as you climb.

Exact ruleBreak-even arms at 0.8R of profit (after the TP0 nibble), moving the stop to entry + a small buffer. CPositionCoordinator.mqh:2904-2937

The trailing stop (Chandelier) — follows price, only ever tightens

This is the headline of trade management. As a buy runs up, the stop follows behind it like a ratchet (a socket wrench or a roller-coaster bar — it clicks one way and physically can't loosen). The level sits a cushion below the recent high, and that cushion is sized by volatility (ATR): tight when gold is calm, wider when it's wild, so a normal swing won't shake the trade out. If price pulls back, the stop stays put.

Exact rulestop = highest recent high − (3 × ATR) for a buy (mirrored for a sell). A new stop is accepted only if it's better than the current one and has moved a minimum amount — strictly ratchet-only. CChandelierTrailing.mqh:146-207 · InpTrailChandelierMult = 3.0

Three other ways a trade ends

Time exit — if a trade just sits there going nowhere for about 3 days, it's closed ("a stale position is a stale opinion"). Weekend close — everything is flattened late Friday so the bot isn't caught across the weekend "gap" when gold reopens at a different price. Regime-aware exit — if the market's whole character clearly flips against the trade (a full 1-hour candle closing through the 50-period average the wrong way — real evidence, not a brief flicker), it bails.

Exact ruleTime exit at InpMaxPositionAgeHours = 72h · weekend close Friday ≥ 20:00 · regime exit needs a closed H1 bar through EMA-50 against the position (not mere chop). CMaxAgeExit.mqh · CPositionCoordinator.mqh:1712 · CRegimeAwareExit.mqh:51-67

Putting it together

8A worked example trade

Illustrative onlyInvented numbers to show the mechanics — not a real trade, not a recommendation, and it says nothing about whether such a trade would win or lose.

Account: $10,000. On gold, 1 point = $0.01, so a $1 move = 100 points.

The hour opens. A fresh 1-hour candle closes. The bot reads the room: long-term trend up, market type trending, the calm part of New York. Good backdrop for a buy.

A setup appears. The just-closed hour printed a bullish engulfing candle near $2,400.00. The pattern's low — the point that would prove the idea wrong — sits at $2,396.00.

It runs the gauntlet. Kill switch off; not halted; no shock; session quality fine; spread normal; not flip-flopping; allowed hour; the setup grades as a solid tier; only 1 of 5 slots in use. All gates: pass.

It sets the stop and checks the reward. Stop just below the pattern low, ~$2,395.50 — a stop distance of about $4.50. The reward must be ≥ 1.3× that, and the laddered targets sit further out, so the check passes.

It sizes the trade from the stop distance. For a solid setup it risks ~1% of equity = $100. With a $4.50 stop, that's about $100 ÷ $4.50 ≈ 22 ounces of gold (~0.22 of a 100-oz lot), rounded down. (The session/regime dials can trim that, and it must fit under the 5% total-risk ceiling — but the idea is constant: risk a small fixed amount, let the stop distance decide the size.)

The order goes in, double-checking spread and price as it fires. Then the management ladder takes over:

  1. Early partial (TP0). At ~0.7R profit (near $2,403.15) it closes 15% and pockets it — the trade has "paid for its parking."
  2. Break-even. At 0.8R it slides the stop up to about the entry price — the original $100 can no longer be given back.
  3. More rungs. If price keeps climbing it books 40% at 1.3R and 30% at 1.8R.
  4. Trail the rest. The runner rides a trailing stop that ratchets up behind price but never loosens — a rope you can pull tighter but never let out.
  5. Final exit. Price eventually turns and taps the trailing stop (or a housekeeping exit fires). The position closes, the result is logged, and the bot goes back to checking the shelf once an hour.

The whole idea in one breath

Read the market, let many scouts compete and pick the single best idea, make it survive a wall of safety checks, size it so a wrong guess costs a small fixed amount, then take profit in slices, get to break-even fast, and trail the rest with a stop that only ever moves in your favor — all decided fresh once an hour, with hard limits that pump the brakes long before the account is in danger.

Reference

9Glossary

Candle / bar
One block on a chart summarizing a chunk of time (here, one hour): where price opened, closed, and its high/low. A "closed" candle is finished.
Pip / point
The smallest standard price step. On this bot's gold, 1 point = $0.01, so a $1 move = 100 points.
Spread
The small gap between the price you can buy at and sell at — a built-in cost of entering a trade.
ATR
Average True Range — how much price typically moves per bar lately; a "how bouncy is it?" meter.
ADX
A meter for how strong/trending the market is (any direction): high = a strong march, low = aimless drifting.
EMA
Exponential Moving Average — a smoothed average line; the 200-EMA is a long-term "are we above the tide line?" reference for whether buying is favored.
Trend / bias
The trend is which way price is generally heading; the bot's bias is the direction it's predisposed to trade (strongly tilted to buying gold).
Regime
The type of market now — trending, ranging, choppy, or volatile. It changes how boldly the bot acts.
Session
A major trading window by region — Asia, London, New York. Gold behaves differently in each.
Support / resistance
Price floors and ceilings — levels where price has tended to bounce (support) or stall and turn (resistance).
Liquidity
How easily you can trade without moving the price; lots = smooth/busy, thin = jumpy/harder.
Stop-loss (SL)
The pre-set "I was wrong, get me out" exit that caps a trade's loss.
Take-profit (TP)
A pre-set price where the bot cashes in some or all of a winning trade.
R / R-multiple
"R" is the amount risked on a trade. Everything is measured in Rs: a 1.3R target is 1.3× the risk; 0.8R profit is up 0.8 of a risk-unit.
Lot
The unit of trade size. A standard gold lot is 100 ounces; the bot trades fractions sized to the risk.
Risk %
The slice of the account the bot will lose on one trade if its stop is hit (~1% for a solid setup, hard-capped at 2%).
Drawdown
How far the account has dropped from its recent peak; the bot brakes as drawdown grows.
SMC
Smart Money Concepts — reading charts around where big institutional orders likely sit and how price hunts those zones; used as one quality check.
