Competition & the Gauntlet

Two links of the chain happen here. First the scouts compete — even if five shout "yes", only the single highest-scoring idea is hired that hour. Then that one winner must clear a wall of twelve safety gates, in order. A "no" at any gate cancels the trade outright. This is the bot's immune system, and it's worth understanding gate by gate.

Choosing one winner

Every "yes" from the panel is scored 0–10 on how well it fits the room, then graded. Only the top-scoring idea per hour survives to face the gauntlet.

The score is built from a handful of honest questions: does the setup agree with the trend across the daily, 4-hour and hourly charts? Does it fit the current market mood (bold in a trend, cautious in chop)? Is the macro backdrop aligned? How strong and clean is the pattern itself? And a penalty is subtracted for choppiness. Those add up to a number, and the number lands the idea in a grade — A+, A, B+, or "rejected."

The grade isn't just a label; it earns the idea the right to risk slightly more money. Here's the mapping (the exact percentages come from the account's configured risk profile):

A+
Top-grade setup
1.35%
A
Strong setup
0.90%
B+
Decent setup
0.68%

Only that single graded winner moves on. Everyone else's idea is dropped until the next hour — which is why the bot rarely opens more than one new trade an hour even when the market is busy. Ties are broken by the raw score, so the "best" is always unambiguous.

The one-hour confirmation

Many winners don't enter immediately — they wait one more hour for a confirmation candle to close in the pattern's favour before committing real money.

A pattern on the just-closed candle is a strong hint, but a hint can still fail. So for most setups the bot waits for the next hourly candle to close past the pattern's trigger level in the intended direction. If that next candle confirms, the trade goes ahead; if it disappoints, the idea is quietly abandoned. Better to miss a trade than take a fake one — this single extra hour of patience filters out a lot of false starts.

Who skips it, and why. Short trades act immediately — for a sell, the "confirming" bounce would usually never come (in a rising market the very next candle tends to pop back up), so waiting is self-defeating. Mean-reversion trades and a few precise scouts (the range and expansion strategies) also trade on their trigger without the extra hour, because their edge is in acting fast at the turn.

The twelve-gate gauntlet

Spotting a trade is only the beginning. The single winning idea must now pass all twelve of these checks, in order — and a single failure cancels it until next hour. The twelve fall into three natural stages.

Stage one — is it even safe to trade right now?

gates 1–7

These seven gates say nothing about the trade idea itself. They ask whether the moment and the machine are fit to trade at all — mostly quick account or clock checks, with a couple that take the market's temperature.

01

Kill switch on?

setting

In plain termsThe operator's master "stop everything" switch. If a human has flipped it off, the bot does nothing — no new trades, no exceptions. It's the big red button on the factory wall.

TechnicallyA single master-enable flag checked at the very top of the routine. When off, the whole signal path is skipped for the bar. No chart, no data — a pure operator setting.

02

Trading halted for the day?

accountclock

In plain termsSome days are just bad, and the bot knows to stop digging. If it has already lost too much today, or hit its cap on the number of trades, it benches itself until tomorrow — like a disciplined gambler who walks away at a preset loss limit.

TechnicallyThe risk monitor tracks the day's equity change and the number of trades opened today. If equity is down about 3% on the day, or the daily trade budget is used up, a halt flag blocks all new entries until the server date rolls over. It watches live equity, so an open loser can trip it intraday.

03

Abnormal price shock?

H1M5live

In plain termsIf gold has just done something violent — a sudden spike or a liquidity air-pocket — the bot steps back rather than trading into chaos. Entering right after a shock is like merging onto a motorway during a pile-up.

TechnicallyA three-part shock detector: it divides the just-closed hourly bar's range by the 4-hour regime ATR, divides a 5-minute "fast-leg" range by the hourly ATR, and compares the live spread to a rolling 20-sample baseline. If any of the three exceeds its threshold, the bar is flagged as shocked and the trade stands aside.

04

Low-quality time to trade?

clockH1live

In plain termsNot every hour fills orders cleanly. If this session has recently shown poor execution — fills at bad prices, thin activity, wide costs — the bot treats it as a low-quality window and passes. Like avoiding the checkout line that keeps jamming.

TechnicallyA session-execution-quality score built from the recent per-session slippage history, the current hour's tick-volume (a liquidity proxy), and the live spread. Below a quality threshold the entry is blocked; nearer the edge it can instead trim the trade's size.

05

Cost too high?

live

In plain termsEvery trade pays a small entry cost called the spread. When that cost is bloated — which happens at quiet hours or around news — the bot refuses, because an oversized cost eats the edge before the trade even starts.

TechnicallyA hard spread gate: the live bid–ask spread in points is compared against a fixed ceiling (the max-spread setting, 50 points on gold). Over the ceiling → reject. A pure live-quote check with no chart read.

06

Market flip-flopping?

H4

In plain termsIf the market can't make up its mind — flipping between "trending" and "choppy" every hour — the bot waits for it to settle. Trading a market that keeps changing character is a fast way to get whipsawed.

TechnicallyA regime-thrash cooldown: it counts how many times the 4-hour regime label has changed recently, and if it has flipped more than twice in a 4-hour span, a cooldown blocks entries until the classification stabilises.

07

Allowed session & hour?

clock · GMT

In plain termsThe bot only trades during certain windows, and specific erratic hours can be switched off entirely. This gate confirms the current time is on the permitted list.

TechnicallyTwo clock checks — the session gate (is the current GMT hour inside an enabled Asia / London / New-York window?) and an optional skip-hours list (specific hours to always avoid; off by default). Both read the server/GMT clock, never a candle.

Stage two — is the idea actually good?

gate 8

A single gate, but the deepest one — and by far the biggest filter in the whole system. This is where the trade idea has to prove its own quality, and where most candidates die.

08

Passes its own quality vetting?

H1H4D1

In plain termsThe idea has to earn its place. Does it agree with the trend on the bigger charts? Does the market's structure back it up? Is the pattern strong and confident, not a half-hearted shape? And does it score a good enough grade? Weak, marginal, or trend-fighting ideas are cut right here — this is the gate that keeps the bot buying genuine strength rather than noise.

TechnicallyA stacked validator known as the "long-bias ladder." It checks the hourly 200-EMA against live price, the alignment of the 4-hour and daily trends, the 4-hour ADX trend-strength, and smart-money structure on the hourly chart. Breakout-class patterns get an extra tick-volume confirmation. It also enforces a minimum pattern confidence and a minimum grade (anything below B+ is rejected).

In the backtestThis one stage removed more candidates than all the others combined — roughly 615 cut for a weak volume signal, 532 for failing the trend/structure ladder, 361 for scoring below grade, and 103 for low pattern confidence over the 7.5-year run.

Stage three — does the trade make sense on paper?

gates 9–12

The idea has passed. Four final sanity checks now confirm the mechanics of the actual trade stack up — its slot, its stop, its reward, and the account's total exposure.

09

Too many positions open?

count

In plain termsThe bot never lets itself get over-committed. It holds at most five trades at once; if all five slots are full, a new idea simply waits, however good it looks.

TechnicallyA hard position-count cap (max 5 concurrent, and at most 5 opened per day). The open-position count is compared to the limit before execution; at the limit, the entry is rejected. Pure count, no chart.

10

Stop safely clear of the noise?

live

In plain termsA stop-loss placed too close to the current price gets clipped by ordinary market jitter, turning a fine trade into a needless loss. This gate makes sure the stop sits far enough away to breathe.

TechnicallyThe distance from entry to the proposed stop must be at least three times the current spread. If the setup's stop is tighter than that, the trade is rejected as too fragile — a live-spread-versus-stop-distance geometry check.

11

Reward worth the risk?

geometry · R

In plain termsBefore committing, the bot checks that the potential reward is meaningfully bigger than the risk — at least 1.3 to 1. A trade that risks a dollar to make a dollar isn't worth the friction and cost.

TechnicallyThe reward-to-risk gate: it measures the distance from entry to the take-profit target against the distance from entry to the stop, and the ratio must be ≥ 1.3. Below that, reject. Pure price geometry — no timeframe involved.

12

Within the total-risk cap?

account %

In plain termsEven five good trades can add up to too much risk at once. This final gate checks the combined risk of everything open plus the new trade; if it would exceed 5% of the account, the new trade is shrunk to fit — or dropped.

TechnicallyThe aggregate-exposure cap. It sums the open risk across all live positions plus the candidate's risk; if the total tops 5% of account equity, the position is either scaled down to fit under the cap or rejected outright.

The gate that ships off

One notable gate is disabled by default: a news filter.

A filter meant to make the bot stand flat around big scheduled announcements — interest-rate decisions, inflation reports, jobs numbers — ships switched off on the default configuration. Those releases can move gold violently in seconds, jumping right through a resting stop-loss.

It's off because it's tied to a broader module that is also off, and because turning it on is a deliberate live-trading decision reviewed on its own merits rather than a default assumption. When enabled, it reads the broker's economic calendar (with a static fallback list) and flattens or stands aside around the highest-impact events. Worth knowing it exists — and that, by default, the bot does not automatically dodge news.

What the gauntlet really does

It's tempting to assume all this filtering must be throwing away a fortune in good trades. The honest answer is the opposite.

Across the 7.5-year test, of roughly 3,300 candidate ideas that reached this stage, the gates and the one-hour confirmation removed about half before a single trade was placed. But when those rejected ideas are priced out — asked "what would you have earned if we'd let you through?" — the answer clusters around break-even. The gauntlet isn't strangling winners; it's removing a large pile of coin-flip trades that would have added churn, cost and drawdown without adding profit.

That's the quiet purpose of the whole wall: not to catch more trades, but to make sure the ones that survive are genuinely worth the risk — and to keep the account calm while it waits. A surviving idea now moves to the most mechanical link of all: deciding how big to bet. That's the next chapter — Sizing the Trade.
