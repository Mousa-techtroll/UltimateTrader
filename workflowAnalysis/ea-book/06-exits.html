Managing the Exit

Getting in is only half the job. Every open trade is managed continuously — not once an hour — against a fixed set of exits: a stop that only ever tightens, a ladder that banks profit in slices, a trailing stop that rides a runner, and three housekeeping exits. The trailing stop is the most involved of these, so this chapter gives it a section of its own.

First, what "R" means

R is simply the amount risked on the trade — the distance from entry to the initial stop, in dollars.

It's the single most useful unit in this chapter. If a trade risks $100 to its stop, then "1R" is $100, "1.3R of profit" is $130, and "break-even" is 0R. Measuring everything in R instead of raw dollars means the same rules work identically whether a trade risked $50 or $500 — the ladder below is written entirely in R, not in prices or time.

Like counting in "bus fares" instead of coins — once you know one fare, every distance is easy to compare, no matter the local currency.

The initial stop

Set by the scout just past its invalidation point — the price that would prove the idea wrong — and never widened once placed.

Every trade is born with a stop-loss. It's parked just beyond the level that would mean the setup has failed — for a bought pin bar, just below the rejection wick; for an engulfing, just under the pattern. But it's never placed closer than a fixed "gold-noise floor" — on the order of 500 points of price — so ordinary hourly jitter can't clip it out of a perfectly good trade.

Two properties make it trustworthy: it defines the 1R risk the whole account was sized against, and after entry it only ever moves toward safety — tighter, never looser. The bot has no mechanism to widen a stop to avoid a loss. If price reaches it, the trade closes. Full stop.

Rather than guess one exit, the bot banks profit in slices and keeps a "runner" to ride a strong trend. Everything below is measured in R, not time.

These rungs shift with the market's mood. The distances above are the normal-market profile. Like the trailing cushion later in this chapter, the take-profit ladder is set per regime: in a clean trend the slices are pushed further out (about +0.7R / +1.5R / +2.2R) to let a winner run; in chop they're pulled in (about +0.5R / +1.0R / +1.4R) to bank faster; volatile sits near the normal profile. Same three-slice shape, regime-tuned spacing.

Why slice it up? Because no one knows in advance whether a trade will stall at +1R or run to +5R. Banking part of the position early locks in a real gain and takes the pressure off; keeping a runner leaves the door open to a big trend. That runner is what the trailing stop then manages — the subject of the two sections below.

A reality check on how often the full ladder runs. These rungs describe what happens if a trade gets there — and most don't. Measured across the 801-position release baseline, about 64% of trades reach the first nibble (+0.7R), about 46% reach the +1.3R slice, and about 29% reach the top rung (+1.8R). The majority never complete the ladder — they ride as a single block to their stop or trailing exit. The ladder is the plan for a winner, not the typical path.

Break-even: playing with the market's money

On the default config there is no active "jump to break-even" at a fixed trigger — instead the trailing stop climbs to the entry on its own as the trade advances.

From that moment the worst case becomes a scratch rather than a loss — the position is "playing with the market's money." In practice this happens hand-in-hand with the trailing system below: as the trailing stop ratchets upward, it naturally carries the stop through the entry price and onward, so "break-even" isn't a separate lever so much as the moment the trail crosses zero. The important guarantee is the same either way — a trade that has run far enough can no longer become a loser. (The bot does contain an explicit "move stop to break-even" lever, but it ships switched off; on the default config break-even is entirely the trailing stop's doing, and — because that stop hangs a wide volatility cushion below the recent high — only a minority of trades ever advance far enough for it to climb all the way to the entry.)

Underpinning everything: the stop is never allowed closer than a minimum gold-noise distance. That floor is what keeps the trailing stop from hugging price so tightly that a single ordinary wiggle shakes the bot out of a good trade too early.

The trailing stop, in detail

A trailing stop is a stop that follows price in your favour and never backward — it ratchets up behind a rising trade to lock in more and more, and closes the trade only when price finally pulls back to it. This is how the bot lets a runner ride a trend without ever giving all the profit back.

What's actually running: the Chandelier

The bot ships with several trailing methods in its toolbox, but only one is switched on and doing the work: the Chandelier trailing stop. The name comes from how it "hangs" the stop down from the highest point the trade has reached, like a chandelier hanging from a ceiling. As the ceiling (the recent high) rises, the chandelier rises with it.

trailing stop (for a buy) = highest high of the last ~15 hours − ( cushion × hourly ATR )

In words: take the highest price the trade has seen over roughly the last 15 hourly candles, then drop down a volatility-sized cushion below it. That cushion is a multiple of the hourly ATR — the "how bouncy is gold right now" gauge from Chapter 2 — so the stop automatically sits close in calm markets and further back in wild ones, instead of a fixed number of dollars that would be too tight in one regime and too loose in another. For a short trade the whole thing mirrors: lowest low + cushion × ATR.

The cushion widens and narrows with the market's mood

The cushion multiplier isn't fixed — it's set by the market regime (Chapter 2), so a runner is given room to breathe in a clean trend and kept on a tighter leash in chop. These are the multipliers currently in use; a wider bar means a wider cushion, i.e. more room before the stop is hit:

Two safeguards stop this from misbehaving. First, the regime that chooses the multiplier is held for about three bars before it's allowed to change, so a one-hour flicker between "trending" and "choppy" doesn't yank the cushion around. Second — and importantly — the stop itself only ever ratchets: a freshly computed level is accepted only when it sits tighter than the stop already in the market. So even if the market later flips to a regime with a smaller cushion, the trail simply holds where it is — a narrower cushion can never pull the stop backward and knife the runner out. The cushion can widen to give a trade room, but a tighter one can never move the exit against you mid-flight.

How it's executed, step by step

The trailing stop isn't a single event — it's a small routine the bot runs over and over. Here is exactly what happens each time it considers moving the stop:

Recompute on each finished hour.

When a new hourly candle closes, the bot recalculates the chandelier level from the latest highest-high and the latest hourly ATR — all from closed candles, so the number can't repaint.

Only accept if it's strictly better.

The new level is compared to the stop already in the market. It's accepted only if it's higher (for a buy) than the current stop — i.e. tighter, more protective. If the computed level is lower, it's ignored. The stop never loosens.

Must clear a minimum step.

Even a better level is skipped unless it moves by a small minimum amount, so the bot doesn't spam the broker with dozens of trivial one-cent adjustments.

Respect the broker's minimum distance.

Brokers won't accept a stop placed too close to the current price. If the computed level is inside that forbidden band, it's nudged out to the nearest allowed distance before being sent.

Send the modification — then wait and watch.

The new stop is written to the live order. Between hourly recomputes it simply sits in the market: if price falls to it at any tick, the runner is closed immediately, whatever the clock says.

A subtle consequence of the "strictly better" rule: early in a trade the chandelier level is usually still below the initial stop, so nothing moves. It only begins to bite once price has advanced enough that "recent high − cushion" climbs above the initial stop — and from then on it ratchets upward, hour after hour, for as long as the trend keeps making new highs.

That's the entire job of the trailing stop — turn a good move into a booked one without ever guessing the top.

One live exception. For the crash-fade short strategy (the "Rubber Band" scout), the ratchet is deliberately held off until a closed hourly candle finishes below its 21-hour average — that is, until the snap-back the trade is waiting for has actually begun. Trailing that particular trade too early was measured to strangle it: its whole thesis is a mean-reversion, so tightening a stop on the initial spike would close it before the move it's built around. (The hard stop and take-profit stay live the entire time — only the trailing ratchet waits.) For every other strategy, the trail runs exactly as described above.

Housekeeping exits

A few housekeeping rules act at the calendar or account level, independent of any single trade's target or trailing stop — a weekend gap to dodge, a stalled range trade to cut, and a bad-day circuit-breaker.

Weekend close

Flatten everything late on Friday to dodge the weekend gap — the jump in price when the market reopens Sunday night that can leap right over a resting stop.

Friday ≥ 20:00 server

Range-trade stall-out

The two range scouts (the Asian-range breakout and its failed-break reversal) get a dead-trade rule of their own: after about 5 fifteen-minute bars still under +0.8R the position is halved and moved to break-even; after about 8 bars still under +1.0R it's closed outright. The candle scouts don't use this — they lean on the stop and trail instead.

S3 / S6 · 5–8 × M15 bars stalled

Daily-loss halt

If the account is down about 3% on the day, the bot stops opening new trades until the next session. It's a circuit-breaker on a bad day rather than an exit on any one position — it caps how deep a single day can dig, and leaves already-open trades to their own stops.

equity −3% on the day

Two exits you might expect are switched off. The code also carries a fixed 72-hour time-out and a regime-flip exit (bail when an hourly candle closes back through the 50-EMA). On the default gold configuration neither is wired to a live owner — they're dormant leftovers of protection that now lives entirely in the stop and the trailing stop. So don't count on a stalled candle-scout trade being auto-closed at the three-day mark, or on a single 50-EMA cross pulling you out: apart from the weekend close and the range stall-out above, a normal trade ends only at its stop or its trailing stop.

How they interact

On a live trade all of these run at once, and whichever fires first wins.

Picture a buy that's going well. It nibbles ~15% at +0.7R; its trailing stop climbs up through break-even on its own around +0.8R as the trade advances; it banks ~40% of the rest at +1.3R and ~30% of the rest at +1.8R. The remaining runner — around a third of the original position — now rides the chandelier trail described above. If gold keeps climbing, the trail keeps ratcheting the stop higher, locking in more and more, until price finally pulls back and taps it. And if it's still open late Friday, the weekend close flattens it regardless of where it sits. Two exits you might reach for play no part on a trade like this: on the default config there is no live 72-hour time-out and no 50-EMA regime-flip exit — a candle-scout position ends only at its stop, its trailing stop, or the Friday close.

That layered design is the heart of the bot's calm temperament: losses are capped at 1R by a stop that never widens, winners are harvested in slices while a runner is left to ride a trailing stop that only ever tightens — and although only a minority of trades climb far enough to reach break-even, the ones that do can no longer become losers. It's why, over the years, the account's dips have stayed roughly half as deep as simply buying and holding gold — the subject that the closing chapter, Start to Finish, ties together on a single worked trade.

On the workbench: a smarter exit brain (not yet live)

One more thing lives in the codebase that this chapter has deliberately left out of the live exits above — because it doesn't touch a single real trade. It is built and tested, but parked in the "off" position.

Dormant · not live Exit-Momentum Platform — built, proven, switched off. A closed-bar momentum reader and a strategy-aware exit engine run in shadow mode — they observe and log only, propose but never trade — and have been proven decision-free and byte-for-byte identical to the live bot on both data feeds, with both master switches off by default. It changes no live trade today.

The momentum reader. Once an hour, and only on candles that have already closed, it takes the market's pulse — is the trend accelerating or running out of steam, is price overextended, is a reversal starting to confirm — and turns those readings into a handful of plain 0–100 scores. Crucially, it never guesses: if a reading can't be computed cleanly it is marked "unavailable" rather than filled in with a made-up number, so no downstream decision can ever rest on a fabricated value.

The strategy-aware exit engine. Instead of one set of exit rules for every trade, it sorts each position into one of five families — trend-continuation, breakout, mean-reversion, reversal, or crash — and then into a profile tuned to the exact scout that opened it, so an engulfing trend trade and a "Rubber Band" crash fade would be managed by different playbooks. But here is the safety spine: these policies only ever propose. They hand the coordinator a suggestion — tighten this stop, close a slice, give the trail more room next time — and the coordinator stays the single piece of code allowed to touch a live order. It will only ever tighten or close, never loosen or widen.

Why you can trust it's really off. The whole platform runs in shadow: it watches every trade and writes down what it would have done, without doing any of it. Two master switches — one to compute-and-log, one to actually act — both ship off, and the backtests reproduce to the exact cent with the platform present but dormant. It is a fully built engine idling in neutral.

Tested, and deliberately not adopted. One profile was even taken out for a measured test drive — widening the trailing cushion on trend trades (internally, TREND_TRAIL_WIDEN_V1). The result was a non-event: a few dollars either way with every risk figure unchanged, because the wide trail it proposed is already the one this chapter's chandelier ships with. So it was tested and not adopted. No profile is live today; if one ever is, it will be switched on a single profile at a time, only once the evidence earns it — never wholesale.
