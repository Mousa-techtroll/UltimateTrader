Backtest Results

The rest of this book explains how the bot works. This appendix shows how it did — the latest full backtest over 7.5 years of real gold price data, how it compares to simply holding gold, and a detailed year-by-year breakdown of every trade, split by direction and by strategy. Every number here is measured from the trade log, not projected; and a backtest is history, never a promise.

How this figure was reached. The number climbed through a series of correctness fixes rather than profit-chasing tweaks — an earlier audited baseline of $21,623.18 was lifted by a crash-strategy exit improvement (+$2,148), a reward-check symmetry fix that freed a handful of blocked short trades (+$85), and finally a daylight-saving fix to the tester's session clock (+$972). Every detailed table below is drawn from this current baseline's own trade-by-trade log — 950 positions, whose raw per-trade profits sum to $25,350 before swap and commission are deducted, i.e. the $24,829.05 headline.

One sub-window worth noting for the sceptical: the most recent stretch (2023 → mid-2026), kept deliberately "out of sample" during development, contributed roughly +$22,500 of the total at a comparable drawdown — the bot's behaviour held up on data it wasn't tuned against.

The bot beat buy-and-hold on raw return — by nearly 30 percentage points — while taking less than half the drawdown and leaving capital idle (and free for other uses) roughly 88% of the time. On a reward-per-unit-of-risk basis it's more than twice as efficient. That efficiency — not the headline return — is the real point of the machine: capture most of gold's trend while feeling far calmer to hold.

An honest caveat. This window happens to end inside gold's 2026 correction. Measured at gold's January-2026 peak instead, buy-and-hold would look far stronger and the return gap would narrow or close. Any "bot vs hold" verdict on a strongly trending asset is sensitive to exactly where you stop the clock — so treat the drawdown and exposure numbers, which are stable, as the durable story, not the return margin.

Two facts jump out. First, three candlestick strategies — Pin Bar, Engulfing and MA Cross — produced 75% of the trades and about 85% of the profit; they are, at heart, three shapes of the same idea (buy a strong hourly candle in an uptrend). Five of the loaded strategies never fired at all — exactly the dormancy Chapter 3 describes: on gold's hourly chart their conditions simply never all lined up.

Note on Expansion: the session-breakout logic that Chapter 3's "Session engine" ships switched off is actually run as a composed sub-strategy inside the Expansion engine — roughly half of Expansion's 26 trades are those London/Asian-range breakouts, tagged under the Expansion name. That is why there is no separate "Session" row here.

The long book and the short book

Split by direction, the asymmetry is stark — and deliberate:

The long book does most of the work — over four-fifths of the profit from 553 buys. The 397 shorts contribute a smaller, steadier fifth: careful fades against a market that spent these years mostly rising. That thin short side isn't an oversight; it's the residue of repeated tests showing that shorting gold over this era lost money almost everywhere except a couple of narrow niches — the death-cross "rubber band" and non-New-York pin bars. The bot shorts where it has an edge and stays out where it doesn't.

This split reveals why the short book, though small, earns its place: it does its best work precisely when the long book struggles. In the choppy 2021 and the 2023 correction, shorts out-earned longs (+$576 vs −$355 in 2021; +$2,170 vs +$1,039 in 2023). The single worst cell was 2019's shorts, winning barely 29.7% — part of why that warm-up year was the only losing one.

The profit matrix is where the character of each strategy shows. Pin Bar and Engulfing carry nearly every year — but even they have red years (Pin Bar lost −$3,455 in the 2026 correction; Engulfing was negative right through 2021–2023). No single strategy is reliable every year; the book works because their good years rarely all fail at once.

Reading the results

What should a non-trader take away from all this? A few honest conclusions.

As a risk-adjusted vehicle, it does its job well. It beat buying gold over 7.5 years — on both total return and compound growth — while suffering less than half the drawdown and keeping most of the account free most of the time. If the goal is "own gold's upside without gold's stomach-churning dips," the numbers support that.

It is a trend-follower, and the calendar shows it. One exceptional year — 2025 — was about 58% of the entire profit, and 2022–2025 together were roughly 95%. In the flat, choppy years (2019 and 2021) it barely moved the needle, and 2019 was a small loss. That's by design: the machine is built to buy strength and protect capital, feasting when gold trends and treading water when it doesn't.

It wins by payoff, not by accuracy. Its win rate never topped 50% in any year — it makes money because its exit ladder lets winners outrun losers. And it leans with the market: mostly short in the bearish stretches, mostly long as gold ran. It banks profit early on purpose, capturing only a modest slice of every move — the direct trade-off for that shallow drawdown.

A backtest replays the past on historical prices. It cannot capture every real-world friction, and it is not a forecast. Past performance does not guarantee future results; nothing here is financial advice.
