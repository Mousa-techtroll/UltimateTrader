Backtest Results

The rest of this book explains how the bot works. This appendix shows how it did — the frozen v1 release baseline, run on 7.5 years of real gold price data, how it compares to simply holding gold, and a detailed year-by-year breakdown of every trade, split by direction and by strategy. Every number here is measured from the trade log, not projected; and a backtest is history, never a promise.

How this figure was reached. The number climbed through a series of quality and regime fixes rather than profit-chasing tweaks. Starting from the audited $24,829 daylight-saving baseline: a crash-strategy freshness gate (fire only on a fresh death-cross) made the short book regime-robust at no cost; an Engulfing death-cross block (don't buy the pattern against a falling market) added +$2,317; a Pullback-Continuation "A-tier" block removed a mis-graded losing cohort for +$2,482; and an Engulfing "A-tier" block — the same mis-grade, in the biggest candle strategy — added +$2,862. Every detailed table below is drawn from this release's trade-by-trade log — 865 positions, whose raw per-trade profits sum to $33,033 before swap and commission are deducted, i.e. the $32,490.33 headline.

One sub-window worth noting for the sceptical: the most recent stretch (2023 → mid-2026), kept deliberately "out of sample" during development, contributed roughly +$28,750 of the total at a comparable drawdown — the bot's behaviour held up on data it wasn't tuned against.

The bot beat buy-and-hold on raw return — by more than 100 percentage points — while taking half the drawdown and leaving capital idle (and free for other uses) roughly 88% of the time. On a reward-per-unit-of-risk basis it's about three times as efficient (23.0 vs 7.7). That efficiency — not the headline return — is the real point of the machine: capture most of gold's trend while feeling far calmer to hold.

An honest caveat. This window happens to end inside gold's 2026 correction. Measured at gold's January-2026 peak instead, buy-and-hold would look far stronger and the return gap would narrow. Any "bot vs hold" verdict on a strongly trending asset is sensitive to exactly where you stop the clock — so treat the drawdown and exposure numbers, which are stable, as the durable story, not the return margin.

The long book does most of the work — over four-fifths of the profit from 467 buys. The 398 shorts contribute a smaller, steadier fifth: careful fades against a market that spent these years mostly rising. That thin short side isn't an oversight; it's the residue of repeated tests showing that shorting gold over this era lost money almost everywhere except a couple of narrow niches — the death-cross "rubber band" (now gated to fire only on a fresh cross) and non-New-York pin bars. The bot shorts where it has an edge and stays out where it doesn't.

Reading the results

What should a non-trader take away from all this? A few honest conclusions.

As a risk-adjusted vehicle, it does its job well. It beat buying gold over 7.5 years — on both total return and compound growth — while suffering half the drawdown and keeping most of the account free most of the time. If the goal is "own gold's upside without gold's stomach-churning dips," the numbers support that.

It is a trend-follower, and the calendar shows it. One exceptional year — 2025 — was about 56% of the entire profit, and 2022–2025 together were roughly 95%. In the flat, choppy years (2019 and 2021) it barely moved the needle, and 2019 was a small loss. That's by design: the machine is built to buy strength and protect capital, feasting when gold trends and treading water when it doesn't.

It wins by payoff, not by accuracy. Its win rate was 45.9% over the whole run and never far above 50% in any year — it makes money because its exit ladder lets winners outrun losers. And it leans with the market: mostly short in the bearish stretches, mostly long as gold ran. It banks profit early on purpose, capturing only a modest slice of every move — the direct trade-off for that shallow drawdown.

It was frozen when the well ran dry. This v1 release is the point where subtractive tuning stopped paying: three interpretable quality gates captured the real, portable edge, and six further audits (candle-strength, tier-risk, the volume filter, exposure caps, the exit ladder, and standalone-entry breadth) each returned a confident "no change." The book was banked there rather than over-fitted further.

A backtest replays the past on historical prices. It cannot capture every real-world friction, and it is not a forecast. Past performance does not guarantee future results; nothing here is financial advice.
