Sizing the Trade

This is the most mechanical link in the chain, and arguably the most important — good sizing keeps a run of losses survivable. The bot decides how much to lose before it decides how much to buy: it fixes the dollars at stake, looks at the stop distance, and works backwards to a position size so that being wrong always costs the same small amount.

Decide the loss first

Like saying "tonight I'll risk at most $100 at this table" before choosing chip sizes.

In plain termsMost beginners size a trade by asking "how many ounces do I want?" This bot asks the opposite question first: "if I'm wrong and my stop is hit, how many dollars am I willing to lose?" That number — a small slice of the account — is fixed before the size is calculated, and everything else follows from it. The size is an output of the risk, never the input.

TechnicallyThis is fixed-fractional risk sizing. Each trade risks a set percentage of the current account balance to its stop-loss. Because that percentage is held constant, the position size floats to match the stop distance — a jumpy setup with a wide stop automatically gets fewer ounces than a tight, precise one, even though both risk the same dollars. Risk stays flat; only exposure flexes.

Like a poker player who sets tonight's budget before sitting down — the cards change hand to hand, but the most they can lose is decided in advance, not discovered at the end.

The formula, worked

The lot-size math reads no candles at all — it's pure money arithmetic from the stop distance and the account balance.

position size = account × risk% ÷ ( stop distance × value-per-point )

TechnicallyEvery term is concrete. Account × risk% is the dollars at stake (e.g. 1% of $10,000 = $100). Stop distance is how far, in price, the entry sits from the stop. Value-per-point is what a one-point move is worth per unit traded — a fixed property of the instrument. Divide the dollars at risk by the dollar-loss-per-unit at the stop, and you get the number of units to trade.

Walk it through with round numbers. Account $10,000, the graded setup allows 1% risk ($100), and the stop sits $4.50 away from the entry:

dollars at risk = $10,000 × 1% = $100 position size = $100 ÷ $4.50 ≈ 22 ounces (~0.22 lot)

In plain termsThe clever part is what that division does automatically. A wider stop forces a smaller position; a tighter stop allows a bigger one — the dollar risk never changes. Flip the stop in this example: if the setup had a wide $9.00 stop instead, the size would halve to ~11 ounces. Same $100 at risk, half the gold. The trader never has to think about ounces at all.

The safety clampsThe raw result is then tidied: rounded down to the broker's smallest tradable step, clamped to a sensible minimum and maximum size, and refused outright if it would need more than 80% of free margin (so a single trade can never consume the account's borrowing room). Only after those checks does an order size actually exist.

The situational dials

The grade sets the base risk. A series of situational dials then nudge it — almost always down — before the size is calculated. Each reads one aspect of the environment and multiplies the base risk.

Notice the deliberate asymmetry as you read: most dials only ever reduce risk, and the two that can raise it are small and capped. The bot is built to get cautious quickly and bold slowly.

×0.50

Session

clock · GMT

In plain termsThe same trade is riskier at some hours than others. The London open is fast and choppy, so the bot halves its size there; New York gets a light trim; the quieter Asian session runs full size.

TechnicallyA session multiplier keyed on the current GMT hour — London ×0.50, New York ×0.90, Asia ×1.0 — applied to the base risk before the position is calculated.

×1.25

Market mood (regime)

H4

In plain termsIn a clean trend the bot is allowed to press a little harder; in a volatile or choppy market it pulls back. This is the main dial that can nudge risk up, and only modestly.

TechnicallyA regime multiplier from the 4-hour classifier — Trending ×1.25, Ranging ≈×1.0, Volatile ×0.75, Choppy ×0.60.

×0.70

Drawdown brake

account

In plain termsAfter a losing streak the bot quietly shrinks its bets, so a cold run does less damage — and it recovers size only as results improve. It never bets bigger to "win it back."

TechnicallyAn equity-curve controller that scales risk down toward ×0.70 during a drawdown and back toward ×1.0 as the curve recovers. It is capped at ×1.0 — it can only ever reduce, never amplify. Reads the account equity curve, no chart.

×0.50

Counter-trend halving

H1

In plain termsIf a trade is fighting the long-term tide — for example a buy placed below the big trend line — the bot halves the bet. It's allowed, but treated as the riskier exception.

TechnicallyIf the trade's direction opposes the hourly 200-EMA relative to live price, risk is multiplied by ×0.50. (The same tide line the bias uses in Chapter 2.)

×1.15

Volatility burst

H1

In plain termsWhen volatility is rapidly expanding, moves travel further and are worth more, so the bot allows a small size boost to make the most of a lively market.

TechnicallyAn ATR-velocity boost of ×1.15 when the hourly ATR is expanding sharply, measured as recent-versus-older true-range over about six bars.

×0.25

Shock & quality trims — with a floor

H4·H1·M5live

In plain termsThe same shock and session-quality checks from the gauntlet can also quietly trim a trade (rather than block it) when conditions are borderline — but there's a hard floor: no pile-up of brakes can shrink a trade below a quarter of its base.

TechnicallyShock and execution-quality multipliers stack with the others, and the combined product of all dials is floored at ×0.25 so the total reduction can never go below a quarter.

Dials that ship off or inert. A few appear in the code but do nothing on the default gold configuration: a consecutive-loss scaler (near-no-op), a short-protection dial (set to ×1.0 — no effect), a Wednesday reducer (a calendar tweak, off), and a trend-quality boost (off). They're mentioned only so the picture is complete.

The hard rails

The dials adjust size; these are absolute limits no setup or dial can override. None read a chart — they watch the account and the clock, and they always win.

≤ 2%
Most the account can lose on any one trade
−3%
Daily-loss halt: stop all new trades for the day
5
Maximum open positions at once (and per day)
≤ 5%
Combined risk across all open trades
5 in a row
Consecutive failed orders trip a safety halt
Master
Operator emergency kill switch

In plain termsThink of the dials as the accelerator and brakes, and these rails as the guardrails and the speed limiter that no amount of pedal can beat. Whatever the setup wants, a single trade can't risk more than 2%, the open trades together can't risk more than 5%, and no more than five can be live at once.

TechnicallyThe 2% per-trade cap is clamped twice — once during sizing and again just before the order is sent — so no dial or rounding can sneak past it. The total-risk cap sums the open risk of every live position plus the candidate and shrinks-to-fit or rejects. The daily-loss halt watches live equity, so an open trade sliding into a loss can trip it intraday, not just closed losses. And the consecutive-error halt stops everything after five failed order attempts in a row — a circuit-breaker against a broken connection or a misbehaving broker feed, not a market signal.

Putting it all together

Here is the whole sizing routine on one trade, from grade to final lot — every multiplier applied in order, then the caps checked.

Say an A+ setup appears during the London session in a trending market, and it's with the long-term tide (not counter-trend). The base risk starts at the A+ grade and the dials adjust it:

StepRunning risk
Base risk — A+ grade1.35%
× London session  (×0.50)0.675%
× Trending regime  (×1.25)0.84%
Counter-trend? no — unchanged0.84%
Drawdown / vol / shock? none active0.84%
Dollars at risk  (of $10,000)≈ $84
position size = $84 ÷ $4.50 stop ≈ 18.7 oz → rounded down to 0.18 lot caps: under 2% per-trade ✓ · under 5% total ✓ · margin ok ✓ → order placed

Two brakes (the halved London size) and one small boost (the trend) net out to about 0.84% — comfortably under the 2% ceiling — and a modest 0.18-lot order results. Change any input and the size moves with it: a New-York A+ setup would size larger (×0.90 instead of ×0.50); the same idea fighting the trend would be halved again; a mid-drawdown version would be trimmed toward ×0.70. The trader sets none of this by hand — it falls out of the rules, the same way every hour.

Note: the 1.35% A+ base used here is the config-of-record value behind the results appendix — it already bakes in the release's ×0.90 tier calibration (a nominal 1.5% grade, dialled to 1.35%). The lower grades follow the same scaling: A 0.90%, B+ 0.675%. So this worked example matches the actual run — no further scaling is applied on top.

What it will never do

Some of the most dangerous behaviours in trading are the ones this bot is built to refuse. These were audited and confirmed in the code.

No martingale (doubling down after losses)
No grid (stacking orders into a loser)
Never adds to a losing trade
Never increases size after a loss
Never widens a stop-loss

Each of these is a classic way accounts blow up. Martingale — doubling the bet after a loss to win it back — turns one bad streak into a wipe-out. A grid stacks more and more orders into a position that's already going the wrong way, compounding the loss. Averaging down into a loser feels like buying a bargain and usually just enlarges the mistake. And widening a stop to avoid taking a loss is how a small, planned loss becomes a large, unplanned one.

The bot forbids all four by construction: every result-based dial multiplies by 1.0 or less (so a loss can only ever shrink the next bet, never grow it), and the stop-loss only ever ratchets toward safety — there is no code path that moves it further away. With the size decided and checked, the order goes in, and the bot's attention shifts entirely to managing the exit.
