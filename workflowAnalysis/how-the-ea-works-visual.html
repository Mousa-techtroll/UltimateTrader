A field manual for a gold-trading machine

The machine that trades gold, one hour at a time.

This is an automated trading program — a "bot" — that buys and sells gold on its own, around the clock. It doesn't guess or react to every price flicker. Once every hour it runs the same disciplined routine: read the market, interview a panel of specialist "scouts", pick the single best idea, put it through a wall of safety checks, size it to a fixed risk, place the order, and then manage it to the exit. This page explains that routine in plain language — no trading background needed.

STAGE 00

One decision, once an hour

The bot's whole life runs on a single clock: the 1-hour candle. It makes exactly one trade decision when each hour finishes — and only acts on a finished hour, so it never chases a half-formed price that might still reverse.

Think of a careful shopper who checks the shelf at the top of each hour instead of fidgeting over every price flicker. When the hour closes, the bot wakes, runs its full routine start to finish, and then goes quiet until the next hour — while continuously managing any trades already open.

Why the hour and not the minute? A one-hour candle is finished business — its four prices (where it opened, its high, its low, where it closed) can never change again. Acting only on finished candles is the bot's core discipline against being faked out. Everything bigger (the daily and 4-hour context) and everything smaller (15- and 5-minute detail) is read in support of that hourly decision.

LINK 01

Reading the room

Before it considers a single trade, the bot spends the first moments of each hour building one shared picture of the market — the way a seasoned trader glances across several screens before deciding anything. That picture has four parts, and together they decide which trades are even allowed, how much to risk, and how suspicious to be.

Nothing about a specific candle pattern is considered yet. This is pure situational awareness — where are we, what kind of market is this, what's the wider world doing, and what time is it? Every part of it is read from finished candles, never the half-formed one still moving, so the read can't be faked out partway through the hour.

The single most important idea here: the bot asks the same question on several charts at once. "Is price trending up?" gets answered separately on the daily (the big tide), the 4-hour (the day's swell) and the 1-hour (the wave in front of you). A trade is on the firmest ground when all three agree — and treated with suspicion when they don't. This is what "multi-timeframe" means, and it runs through all four reads below.

A

Direction bias — which way am I allowed to lean?

D1H4H1
In plain terms

Gold has trended upward for years, so the bot leans hard toward buying. But "leaning toward buying" isn't a hunch — it's a stacked measurement. The bot asks "is price trending up?" on three clocks at the same time: the daily chart (weeks of direction), the 4-hour (days), and the 1-hour (hours). When all three point up, a buy stands on firm ground; when they disagree, the idea is treated as suspect. Selling is the rare, high-bar exception.

Like a surfer reading the ocean's tide, the day's swell, and the wave right in front of them before paddling out — you want all three working with you, not against.

Under the hood

On each of the three timeframes it runs the same pair of moving averages — a fast 10-period and a slow 21-period EMA — plus a 14-period ATR volatility reading, all on the last closed bar.

An EMA (exponential moving average) is a smoothed average that weights recent candles more heavily, so it tracks direction without the jitter. When the fast 10 sits above the slow 21 and both point up, that timeframe reads "uptrend." Separately, a long-term 200-period EMA on the 1-hour chart is the master "are we above water?" tide line: price above it favours buying; a buy attempted below it gets its size halved.

This bias is the foundation of the whole book — the book runs roughly half long, half short by count, yet longs carry ~80% of the net profit, and the design still holds the sell side to stricter conditions. It powers the main quality gate (the "buy-bias ladder"), and the hourly 200-EMA drives the counter-trend size-halving dial.

B

Market mood — what kind of market is this?

H4
In plain terms

The very same trade that's smart in a clean trend is reckless in chop. So before it sizes anything, the bot names the current market as one of four moods:

Trending — a clean staircase, one step after another. Ranging — boxed between a floor and a ceiling. Choppy — messy and full of head-fakes. Volatile — big, fast, violent lurches.

Like a driver reading the road — open highway, city grid, gravel, or black ice — and setting their speed to match. The mood decides how boldly the bot is allowed to act.

Under the hood

Decided entirely on the 4-hour chart, from three readings of the last closed 4-hour bar:

ADX (14) — how strong the trend is, in any direction (high = a determined march, low = aimless drift; it says how hard, not which way). ATR (14) — the raw "bounciness," how far price travels per bar. Bollinger Bands (20, 2.0) — an envelope that hugs price and widens when volatility rises; riding a band means the market is stretched.

A "volatility is expanding" flag trips when the current 4-hour ATR is more than 1.3× its own 50-bar average — i.e. things just got livelier than usual.

The regime is a master dial. It scales risk directly (Trending ×1.25 · Volatile ×0.75 · Choppy ×0.60), it gates whole scouts (the Volatility Breakout only fires in a Volatile market; the Crash engine needs a confirmed downtrend), and it sets how wide the trailing stop rides a winner.

C

Macro backdrop — is the wider world with me?

H4
In plain terms

Gold doesn't move in a vacuum. It usually rises when the US dollar falls, and it often catches a bid when fear rises across markets. So the bot glances at two outside gauges — the dollar's strength and a market "fear index" — to check whether its intended direction fits the wider weather.

On the default settings this is a gentle nudge, not a veto — it can make a setup look a little better or a little worse, but it rarely blocks a trade outright.

Like checking the forecast before a long drive: it won't cancel the trip, but it changes how cautiously you set out.

Under the hood

It reads the US Dollar Index (DXY) and the VIX "fear gauge" on the 4-hour chart. Because gold and the dollar tend to move opposite each other, a weakening dollar is a tailwind for buying gold; a spike in the fear index often lines up with gold catching a bid.

If those two feeds aren't available from the broker, it falls back to a home-grown proxy: the daily 200-EMA plus the 4-hour 20/50 EMAs measured against live price — a rough stand-in for "is the macro tide with me?"

Macro alignment is one ingredient in the 0–10 quality score that grades every idea. It seldom vetoes a trade by itself, but it nudges the grade up or down — and the grade sets the size.

D

Time of day — which market am I in right now?

ClockM15
In plain terms

Gold trades nearly 24 hours, but it behaves like three different markets across the day: the quiet Asian hours, the busy London open, and the US New York session. Where a move happens matters as much as what it is — a breakout in thin Asian liquidity is a very different animal from one at the London open.

So the bot tracks which session it's in and adapts, mostly by taking less risk in the busier, choppier hours.

Like a cab driver who knows the same street is calm at dawn and chaos at rush hour — and drives accordingly.

Under the hood

The session clock is the broker's server time minus an auto-detected GMT offset (with a sensible fallback). From that it knows whether it's Asia, London or New York. During the overnight window (roughly GMT 0–7) it builds the Asian high–low range from 15-minute candles — a reference box several time-based strategies key off.

Session sets a hard risk multiplier: London ×0.50 (half size), New York ×0.90, Asia ×1.0 — and gates a couple of scouts (bearish pin bars blocked in New York; the crash fade fires at all hours). Plus a weekly clock rule: no new trade is opened on a Friday, to avoid carrying fresh risk over the weekend gap — the price jump when the market reopens Sunday night that can leap right over a stop.

The session is both a risk dial and a gate. It's why the same setup is sized differently at 03:00 versus 09:00 GMT, and why some short trades simply aren't allowed in the US afternoon.

Only once all four reads are in — a favoured direction, a named market mood, a macro tilt, and the session's rules — does the bot turn to the scouts. Everything that follows is filtered through this one shared picture of the room.

The scouts — many specialists, one hire

Picture the bot as a hiring manager who, once an hour, interviews a panel of specialist scouts. Each is an expert in exactly one chart shape. The manager asks them all "do you see your setup?", scores every yes, and hires the single best. Below is every scout, grouped by family — what each one looks for, how it works, and why it's a sound idea.

Candle-shape scouts

4 scouts · the workhorses

The simplest and most reliable readers: they judge the market from the shape of the just-finished hourly candle, always in agreement with the bigger 4-hour trend. Three of these do the overwhelming majority of the bot's actual trading.

Pin Bar

Active

A candle with a long tail and a small body — price stabbed one way and got slapped straight back.

During the hour, price pushed hard in one direction, then reversed and closed near where it started, leaving a long "rejection wick". The bot buys when the tail points down (sellers were rejected) inside an uptrend, and sells the mirror image — but never during the New York session, where short pin bars historically misfire.

Why it works: a long rejection wick marks the exact spot where a push ran out of buyers or sellers. Entering as the reversal confirms lets the bot ride the bounce with a tight, logical stop just past the rejected extreme.

Displacement

Dormant

A stop-hunt followed by a big, decisive drive the other way.

Price first dips below a recent low to trip other traders' stop orders, then a large "power candle" closes back above it — the signature of big money grabbing cheap liquidity and then revealing its true direction. The bot enters after the trap, using the failed level as its stop.

Range scouts

2 scouts · the sharpshooters

These read a faster 15-minute chart to time reversals at the edges of a range, then size the trade from the 1-hour volatility. They fire rarely, but when they do their setups are among the highest-quality in the book.

Range-Edge Fade (S3)

Dormant

A false breakout at the edge of a box — price pokes out, then snaps back in.

When gold is boxed inside a range, price often pokes just past the floor or ceiling to trigger breakout traders, then fails and snaps back inside. On the 15-minute chart, this scout fades that failure back toward the middle of the box, targeting the opposite edge.

Why it's a good idea: most range breakouts fail. Fading the failure at the very edge gives a naturally good reward-to-risk (at least 2:1 to the far side). But its full checklist has to line up perfectly, which has never once happened on gold in the test window.

Breakout scouts

2 scouts · the specialists

These wait for price to break out of its recent range with force. One is a pure-trend breakout kept for volatile markets; the other is the bot's only dedicated bear-market engine.

Volatility Breakout

Dormant

A clean punch out of the recent 20-bar range while the trend agrees.

Price closes beyond both its recent high and a volatility band, during a genuinely volatile market, with a strong trend reading and the 4-hour averages stacked in agreement. A high bar to clear on purpose.

Why it's a good idea: real range-expansion moves are worth catching. But it is deliberately gated to only volatile regimes, which makes it a rare specialist — effectively a reserve that almost never activates.

Crash Breakout (Rubber Band)

Active

A fade of an over-stretched bounce inside a confirmed downtrend.

This is the one scout built for falling markets. It only wakes when the daily 50-day average is below the 200-day average — a confirmed "death cross" downtrend. An honest word on "twelve scouts"

Twelve strategies are loaded, but that's a fact about the roster, not the playing time. Over 7.5 years of real gold data, five of the twelve produced zero trades — their internal conditions are simply too demanding to fire on this market, even with their switches on. The live book is really about seven scouts, and three of them — Pin Bar, Engulfing and MA Cross — do the overwhelming majority of the work.

That isn't a flaw so much as honesty about what actually earns: the bot is, at heart, a disciplined way to buy bullish hourly momentum candles when gold is trending up, wrapped in a great deal of machinery that keeps it out of trouble the rest of the time.

By count, the fills land roughly half Pin Bars, about a fifth Crash-fade shorts, one in seven Engulfing, a few percent MA Cross, and the rest small tails from Pullback Continuation and Expansion.

LINKS 03–04

The competition, then the gauntlet

Two links happen here. First the scouts compete — even if five shout "yes", only the single highest-scoring idea is hired that hour. Then that one winner must clear a wall of twelve safety gates. A "no" at any gate cancels the trade outright.

First, one winner is chosen

Each idea is scored 0–10 on how well it fits the room — trend agreement, market mood, macro alignment, pattern strength, and a choppiness penalty — then graded A+ / A / B+ (or rejected). Only the top-scoring idea per hour is carried forward. A higher grade also earns the right to risk slightly more money, which is where the next link, sizing, comes in.

Many winners then wait one more hour for a confirmation candle to close past the pattern before entering — an extra patience check. Short trades and mean-reversion trades skip this and act immediately.

Then, the twelve-gate gauntlet

Spotting a trade is only the beginning. The winning idea must pass every one of these, in order. One failure and it's dropped until next hour.

Kill switch on?

The operator's master off-switch. If flipped, nothing trades.

Trading halted for the day?

A daily-loss or max-trades halt stands the bot down until tomorrow.

Abnormal price shock?

Stand aside on a violent spike — measured across the hourly, 5-minute and live spread.

Low-quality time to trade?

Checks this session's recent execution quality — slippage, thin volume, wide spreads.

Cost too high?

Reject if the live spread (the built-in cost of entering) is above the limit.

Market flip-flopping?

A cooldown if the 4-hour mood has changed too many times too fast.

Allowed session & hour?

Only trade inside permitted regional windows and non-skipped hours.

Passes its own quality vetting?

The buy-bias ladder: agreement across the daily, 4-hour and hourly trend, plus structure checks.

Too many positions open?

A hard cap of five open trades at once. If full, wait.

Stop safely clear of the noise?

The stop-loss must sit at least three spreads away, so ordinary jitter can't clip it.

Reward worth the risk?

The potential gain must be at least 1.3× the amount risked, or it's not worth taking.

Within the total-risk cap?

All open trades combined must risk no more than 5% of the account. Shrink to fit, or reject.

One notable gate ships off: a news filter that would stand the bot flat around scheduled announcements (rate decisions, inflation, jobs). On the default settings it is disabled — a deliberate choice reviewed separately for live use.

LINK 05

Sizing — decide the loss first

Like saying "tonight I'll risk at most $100 at this table" before choosing chip sizes. The bot fixes the dollars it's willing to lose, looks at how far away the stop is, and works backwards to a position size — so a stop-out always costs that same small amount, no matter the trade.

The formula, in words

no chart read — pure arithmetic

position size = (account × risk%)     ÷ stop distance

A wider stop forces a smaller position; a tighter stop allows a bigger one — the dollar risk stays constant either way. The grade sets the base risk %; a series of situational dials then almost always nudges it down before the order is sized.

LINK 07

Managing the trade to the exit

Getting in is only half the job. Every open trade is managed continuously — not once an hour — against a fixed set of exits: a stop that only ever tightens, a ladder that banks profit in slices, a trailing stop that rides a runner, and three housekeeping exits. First, one key term: R is simply the amount risked on the trade. A "1.3R" target means a gain of 1.3× that risk.

Following the trade

Two mechanisms actively track an open winner — one protects, one chases.

Initial stop-loss

Set by the scout just past its invalidation point — the price that would prove the idea wrong. It's never placed closer than a fixed gold-noise floor, and after entry it only ratchets toward safety. It is never widened.

only moves the safe way

Chandelier trail

The runner's stop follows price up like a ratchet, always sitting a volatility-sized cushion below the recent high. How wide that cushion is depends on the market mood — from about 3× the hourly volatility in choppy conditions out to 4.2× in a clean trend, giving a strong trend more room to breathe. It only ever tightens — locking in more of a strong move as it extends, and stepping out only when price finally pulls back to it.

recent high − (3.0–4.2 × ATR), by regime

Break-even — implicit

The trade becomes risk-free without a dedicated step: once it has a cushion, the trailing ratchet has already climbed the stop to about the entry. A separate break-even mover lives in the code but ships switched off, so break-even here is a by-product of the trail, not its own action.

ON THE BENCH

Built, proven — and deliberately switched off

Everything above is the bot as it trades today. This last section is different: a whole second layer of trade-management intelligence that has been built and tested but ships dormant. It observes and logs only — on the default settings it changes not one live trade.

DormantThe exit-momentum platform

A closed-bar momentum-reading feature and a strategy-aware exit engine (five policy families resolving into per-signal profiles) run alongside the live bot in shadow mode — they watch each trade and record what they would have done, but the position coordinator remains the sole owner of every broker action; these policies only ever propose, never trade. Both master switches ship off, and with them off the program is byte-for-byte identical to the live version on both independent data feeds.

One activation experiment — a slightly wider trend trail — was measured and not adopted (it moved the bottom line by only a few dollars, because the wide trail it proposed is already shipped in the live trailing stop). No profile is live. Activation, if it ever happens, is evidence-gated and one profile at a time.

Plain-English glossary every term＋
Candle / bar
One block summarizing a slice of time by four prices: where it opened, its high, its low, and where it closed. "Closed" = finished and fixed; "forming" = still moving.
Timeframe
How much time each candle covers — M5 (5-min), M15 (15-min), H1 (hourly), H4 (4-hour), D1 (daily), W1 (weekly).
Long / short
Long = betting price goes up (buying). Short = betting price goes down (selling).
Trend / bias
The trend is where price is heading; the bot's bias is the direction it's predisposed to trade — heavily tilted toward buying gold.
Regime
The type of market right now: trending, ranging, choppy, or volatile — decided on the 4-hour chart.
Session
A regional trading window — Asia, London, New York. A clock concept, not a chart.
Spread
The small gap between the buy and sell price — a built-in cost every time you enter.
ATR
Average True Range — how much price typically moves per bar lately. In short: "how bouncy is it?"
ADX
A gauge of how strong a trend is (in any direction). High = a strong march; low = aimless drifting.
EMA / moving average
A smoothed average price line. The 200-period average is a long-term "tide line" for the big picture.
Stop-loss
The pre-set "I was wrong, get me out" exit that caps a loss. This bot's stop only ever tightens.
Take-profit
A pre-set price where the bot cashes in some or all of a winning trade.
R / R-multiple
"R" is the amount risked on a trade. Targets and break-even are measured in R — a 1.3R target is a gain of 1.3× the risk.
Lot / size
The trade-size unit. A standard gold lot is 100 ounces; the bot trades fractions, sized to the risk.
Drawdown
How far the account has fallen from its recent peak. The bot brakes its risk as drawdown grows.
Liquidity / stop-hunt
Liquidity is how easily you can trade without moving price. A "stop-hunt" is a poke past a level to trigger others' stops before reversing.
Death cross
When a shorter average (50-day) falls below a longer one (200-day) — a classic confirmed-downtrend signal.
Smart-money (SMC)
Reading the chart around where large institutional orders likely sit — order blocks, gaps, sweeps. The bot computes these for context, but the SMC "confluence" check is a dead no-op on the shipped build — it always passes and changes no trade.
